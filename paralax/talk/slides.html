Paralax: your own assistant, reading what the group knows

Five people, five assistants, one shared workspace, and a switch. Two benchmarks, a live server, and an honest hill-climb.

→ next · ← back · n speaker notes

A small algorithm with a measured result. When several people work on one problem with their own AI assistants, the assistants can pool what the people know, and the group decides better than people left alone. Where the idea runs out, we measured too.

The problem groups have

  • Groups talk about what everyone already knows. The fact that settles a decision is often held by one person, and nobody knows to ask.
  • Across 65 studies, groups mentioned shared information about two standard deviations more often than unique information; groups facing a hidden profile were eight times less likely to be right. (Stasser & Titus 1985; Lu, Yuan & McLeod 2012)
  • LLM agent groups fail the same way: 30% accuracy against 81% with full information. (HiddenBench, Li, Naito & Shirado 2026)
  • An LLM facilitator in a human chat got people to share 2.9 more facts and did not change their decisions. (Alsobay et al. 2025)
Forty years old, and it survives the move to machines. And it is not a failure of availability: facts that arrive are not facts that get used.

The setting

  • A research department: people with different roles, comprehension and habits of sharing.
  • Each person already talks to an AI assistant. The conversations are private.
  • What if every assistant could read every conversation, and use it only when it helps its own person?

"I don't want to see this kind of detailed information about what the other person is doing."

The constraint that shaped everything: each person must still feel they are talking to their own assistant. No notifications about others, no shared board, no unprompted messages. We built those first, and the person who asked for the system rejected them within a day.

The algorithm

1 · WorkspaceThe ordered record of what every person typed: author, turn, text. No assistant text, no extraction, no summary.
2 · SwitchBefore each reply, one background call: does anything here bear on what my person is working on, given that they are weighing, choosing, asserting or about to act? Closes only for greetings, small talk and setup.
3 · ReadIf it opens, the assistant reads the whole workspace as raw attributed text.
4 · ReplyOne picture organised by the courses of action the person is weighing. Credit by name. A report stays a report, a supposition stays a supposition. Facts and plans, never others' preferences. Optionally one question asking the person for a fact someone else needs.
5 · Decision roundWhen the session ends, each person states a final choice to their assistant.
Switch closedSay nothing about the others.

Two model calls per reply. The only decision the channel makes is whether to open.

That is the result of the talk. The next slide draws it; then how we got there.

The algorithm, as a picture

Follow one person, A. What A types joins the workspace. Before A's assistant replies, the switch reads A's recent conversation and the workspace and decides whether anything bears on what A is doing. If so, the assistant reads all of it and answers A with one attributed picture. If not, the reply says nothing about the others.

Why those rules

  • Communicate when the value to the team exceeds the cost. The query is the person's situation, not the assistant's uncertainty. (Tambe 1997; Marschak & Radner 1972)
  • Err open: an unneeded read costs tokens, a missed read costs the decision. (Horvitz 1999)
  • Broadcast everything relevant and let the knowledge sources judge. (Hearsay-II, Erman et al. 1980)
  • Summaries are where AI mediators steer groups; knowing others' preferences makes group decisions worse. So: primary text, facts only. (Parisi et al. 2026; Mojzisch & Schulz-Hardt 2010)
  • Attribution is the first thing to break in multi-party memory. So: author and turn on every item. (EverMemBench 2026)

Two benchmarks

HiddenBench (Li, Naito & Shirado 2026): 65 group-decision tasks. Shared facts point to a decoy; hidden facts, one per person, point to the right option.

Calibration: one call with full information solves 57 of 65; with the shared facts only, 2 of 65.

The Alsobay task (Alsobay, Rothschild, Hofman & Goldstein 2025): a real human study, 1,475 people in 281 groups, choosing a host city from five different reports. Each report alone favours the bait; pooled, the right city wins. Humans chose it 31% of the time unaided, 23% with a GPT-4o facilitator in the chat. Their stimuli are public.

Our people: five flash-lite personas with department roles: a professor who decides early, an evidence-first postdoc, a first-year who misses implications, a literal lab manager, a talkative visitor from another field.

Each describes the problem to its own flash assistant in its own words. The assistants and the workspace are never given the task.

A battery and one hard instance with a human reference number. Everything runs on both, paired on the same seeds; nothing is adopted that regresses either.

Pooling through the assistants takes a group from 14% to 83%. Against the same people simply talking in one chat, the gain is small: these simulated people share freely, so the human hidden-profile bias is not reproduced. The structural difference stands, private conversations and restraint; the accuracy claim against direct talk is open.

What helps is specific: a reply that carried someone else's hidden fact moved a not-yet-right person one time in three. The two-round lag on the right is the mechanism: type, carry, take in.

What we had to take away

  • v1 at most two items per turn, never repeated: 0.63
  • v2 to v4 re-delivery, wider selection: small gains
  • v5 no relation labels, no reasons: 0.70, late flips stop
  • v6 gate becomes a switch, read it all: 0.79
  • v7 switch closes only for small talk: 0.83
  • Why: with v1, people who received every fact were right 54% of the time; with everything in context, 89%. Delivery is not use. And labels pushed people off correct leanings.
Accuracy rose with how much of the workspace reached the assistant. Every piece of machinery between the workspace and the reader cost accuracy: the cap, the ledger, the labels, the reasons. The one that survived is the switch. What it buys: on ten off-task messages, everything-in-context relayed the other person's report ten times; Paralax three.

The Alsobay task: where Paralax falls short

  • Simulated groups unaided: 50% (humans 31%). Their LLM facilitator, which did nothing for humans, works on simulated people: 80%.
  • Paralax: about 50%, on 18 seeds.
  • The facts reach the workspace: 29 of 30 per session.
  • People follow their assistant: 20 of 21 whose assistant favoured the right city chose it.
  • But a third of the assistants' final replies favour the bait, with all the facts in front of them.
This is the honest slide. The pooling works; the integration does not. The facilitator's public scoreboard beats our private assistants here. So we tried to fix integration, three ways, each a single general rule, each run on both benchmarks with the same seeds.

Three attempts, and why they failed

v8 counted facts: many small positives outweighed one disqualifier, workarounds entered the 'for' column, and relayed votes steered people. v9 eliminated the unworkable first, Tversky's rule: a big gain on ten seeds that vanished on the next eight. v10 wrote one neutral brief without anyone's leaning in view: groups converged sooner, sometimes on the decoy, and the brief read the facts the same bait-favouring way, so the leaning was not the cause.

Replicate before adopting

  • Ten seeds of a five-person task cannot distinguish differences under about 20 points.
  • v9's +22 on the Alsobay task became +11 on 18 seeds (p = .19); its −4 on HiddenBench became +3 on 45 pairs (p = .33). Seed luck of the same size, both ways.
  • The adopted algorithm is unchanged. The open problem is the integrator's reasoning when facts differ in weight.
The winner's curse is real and it is quick. Every candidate now gets replicated on both benchmarks before anything changes.

Scaling

Two calls per reply at any N; tokens grow with (N − 1) × turns × message length. Up to ~60 items: read it all. Up to ~600: retrieve over a claims index with pointers to the turns. Beyond: tiered memory with provenance.

Using the shared server

One command on the host's Mac, deploy/meeting.sh 10, prints a join link and a researcher link. Each click on the join link takes the next free seat. HTTPS end to end, nothing inbound, no login; keys and tokens never leave the Mac. Say at the start: everyone's assistant reads what everyone types, and only the host sees everything.

Next steps

  1. The integrator's reasoning. The Alsobay gap is a reasoning failure with full information in view: a stronger reply model, a verification pass on the reply's conclusion, a "what would change my mind" step. Each gets both benchmarks and replication.
  2. Larger groups and longer sessions. One fact per person at 10 and 20 people; facts drip-fed over 20 and 50 rounds; a seminar room with airtime and attention limits, where direct talk is expected to degrade and Paralax to hold. (Diehl & Stroebe 1987; Gallupe et al. 1992)
  3. People. The Alsobay task on their open platform with real department members, pre-registered on the outcome their facilitator did not move.
  4. Deployment. Sign-in and consent on the server, then Firestore and a Cloud Function inside BOLD OS. The algorithm does not change.

Take-aways

  • Every assistant reading every conversation is a mediator nobody has to talk to.
  • Getting facts to people is not the problem. One integrated picture is, and on hard tasks the integrator itself is.
  • The channel should decide whether to open, and nothing else.
  • Replicate on two benchmarks before adopting anything.
