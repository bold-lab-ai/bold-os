−8 wk

Register the Paper

You need the abstract, a paper outline, and an estimate of the compute the paper will need — not the full text yet.

Pick the venue

  • By default, we are a core-ML lab. The bulk of submissions should go to ICML, NeurIPS, and ICLR.
  • These three deadlines are nicely spaced out (Feb / May / September), so you should never be far from a deadline.
  • Specialised conferences (e.g. AAMAS) are fine if justified.
  • AAAI is usually a last resort.

Register it

  • Add the paper to the Internal Review Board — create the venue's board if it isn't there yet — with the title, abstract, author list, target venue, and a compute estimate. The card starts in Register.
  • When you set the venue's paper deadline, the board derives every internal deadline — register, Pitch Day, first draft, final draft, PI approval — and offers them as a calendar file (.ics) to import.

Clear your calendar

  • If you're the main author, clear your schedule of everything else for the run‑up to the deadline.
−6 wk

Pitch Day

Every paper is pitched to the group on a fixed Pitch Day — everyone presents. A paper that hasn't been pitched by this point doesn't go forward.

  • Prepare a short pitch: the problem, the idea, what the results look like so far.
−4 wk

First Draft & Internal Review

There must be a first full draft. Read and closely follow the “How to ML Paper” guide.

  • Move the card to Draft & Review on the Internal Review Board (you registered it in Phase 1).
  • Assign a junior and a senior reviewer from the pool to the card, then the draft goes through Internal Review on the card itself — a simulated full peer review (venues are all similar). Both the Format and Science parts are mandatory; the paper can't be approved until both reviewers have completed the checklist.
  • Look after yourself. Exercise, sleep, and food matter through the push. A PhD is a marathon, not a sprint.
−2 wk

Final Draft

The final draft is done — every comment from the Internal Review is incorporated.

−1 wk

PI Approval

The PI reads the final draft and approves it for submission. This is the PI's only involvement in the process.

deadline

Submit

Submit before the deadline, following whatever the venue's system requires. But submitting isn't the finish line — it's one small step, not a pause. Reviews take weeks, and that time is some of the most valuable in the whole project. Keep working.

Capture the loose ends

Credit to Roger Grosse for this practice.

“Now that the deadline is past, there's one more (hopefully easy) task for all of you. After a deadline push, I find it very useful to make a list of all the loose ends in the project. It's important to do it very soon after the deadline while everything is still fresh in your mind. This will save a lot of time and stress later on.”

Sometime in the next few days, start a Google Doc to track loose ends. Aim to spend about an hour on it — or build it up as you go if you're doing heavy work for the Supplemental Material deadline.

Ideas for what to include

  • Experiments you still want to run
  • Surprising findings that need to be explained
  • Aspects of the experimental protocol you're not comfortable with
  • References to prior work you need to look at more carefully
  • Things that need to be explained better
  • Mathematical arguments that need to be checked or made more precise
  • Questions about notation / terminology

Release on arXiv & Publicity

Once the paper is submitted and you've made your plan of extensions to work on during review (Phase 6), the next step is to post it on arXiv. This is often the only version many readers ever see — treat it with real care.

arXiv

  • Give co-authors a week's heads-up and share a final draft before posting.
  • The final draft must include author names and affiliations, plus acknowledgments covering grants and funding bodies — the latter is crucial. See the current default here.
  • When listing your affiliation, use “BOLD, {University of Oxford / Imperial College / UCL}” whenever possible.
  • Print off the paper to check for final typos, formatting, and other issues.
  • Open-source your cleaned-up code and link the repo before you submit to arXiv.

Publicity

  • Draft a tweet thread and share it with all authors ahead of time. Typefully can be a useful tool for this.
  • Tweets should clearly motivate the problem for a broad audience and make it easy to grasp what's happening.
  • Example tweet chains from BOLD and collaborators:
    • Chris Lu
    • Minqi Jiang
    • Jakob Foerster
  • Remember to tag @BOLD_Ox and your co-authors.
  • Consider a blog post for major breakthroughs — this is the gold standard.

Rebuttal

Follow the “How to ML Rebuttal” guide closely. The venue's reviews-released and rebuttal-deadline dates, when known, are on the Internal Review Board and in its deadline .ics.

Pre‑Conference

Poster

  • Make a poster and share it with co-authors.
  • Example posters to fork: Jakob's poster and one more.
  • Focus on images and getting the main ideas across.
  • Note of caution: posters are not papers — they're not meant to be self-explaining. You'll be standing there explaining them to people.

Talk

  • Schedule a practice talk with the group if you're presenting.
  • Slides can be Google Slides or an HTML deck — whatever's easiest to build and share with co-authors.

Networking

  • Email anyone you want to talk to at the conference at least 3 weeks beforehand to set up a meeting.
  • Join as many of the conference's social events as you can — this is mandatory. They're usually the best networking.
